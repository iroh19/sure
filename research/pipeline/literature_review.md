### Batch 6 (searches 25–28): Closing gaps — tracking algorithm, formal safety-standard citation, aquaculture economics, RAG evaluation survey

25. **Zhang, Y. et al., "ByteTrack: Multi-Object Tracking by Associating Every Detection Box," ECCV 2022 (arXiv:2110.06864).** Problem: standard tracking-by-detection MOT pipelines discard low-confidence detection boxes, losing true objects under occlusion and fragmenting trajectories. Method: a two-stage cascaded association — first associate high-score detections to existing tracks by motion/appearance similarity, then separately associate remaining low-score detections to still-unmatched tracks by motion similarity alone, recovering occluded/partially-visible objects instead of discarding them. Key results: reported consistent IDF1 improvements of 1–10 points when applied on top of 9 different SOTA trackers; became one of the most widely adopted MOT association methods. Limitations: association-only method — depends entirely on the upstream detector's recall; does not itself improve detection accuracy. Relevance to S.U.R.E.: this is the origin paper for the tracking component of the vision-service (YOLOv11s + ByteTrack); citing it directly grounds the "and ByteTrack" half of the vision pipeline in its original source rather than treating it as an unattributed implementation detail, and its own stated motivation (recovering low-confidence/occluded detections) is directly relevant to H7's discussion of the system's residual per-instance miss rate in dense/occluded frames — ByteTrack mitigates but does not eliminate the occlusion-driven recall gap S.U.R.E. reports.

**Theme 5 completion.** Literature on LLM-agent evaluation reliability independently documents exactly the class of artifact S.U.R.E.'s `bench_agent.py` catches (a model producing a "constant answer" that inflates an aggregate percentage): "Artifacts or Abduction: How Do LLMs Answer Multiple-Choice Questions Without the Question?" (arXiv:2402.12483) shows LLMs (especially larger ones) exploit choices-only/majority-class artifacts that inflate benchmark accuracy without genuine task competence, and general LLM-benchmark-artifact literature (majority-class bias, judge bias, data contamination) treats "the aggregate score looks fine but is a detection artifact" as a known, named failure category in NLP evaluation broadly.

21. **"Artifacts or Abduction: How Do LLMs Answer Multiple-Choice Questions Without the Question?," arXiv:2402.12483, 2024.** Problem: whether LLM multiple-choice benchmark accuracy reflects genuine reasoning or exploitable answer-distribution artifacts. Method: choices-only ablation (removing the question, keeping only answer options) across 12 benchmark/model combinations. Key results: in 11/12 cases, choices-only prompts scored significantly above the majority-class baseline, and larger models exploited these artifacts more, not less — meaning higher headline accuracy does not imply less artifact-reliance. Limitations: multiple-choice QA setting, not agentic tool-selection; a different task structure than S.U.R.E.'s agent benchmark. Relevance: **this is PARTIAL prior art for H3's secondary "constant-answer-detection" methodological contribution.** The general principle — that an aggregate accuracy/selection percentage can be inflated by a model defaulting to one answer regardless of input, and that this must be actively checked for rather than trusted — is an established concern in the NLP-benchmark-artifact literature (majority-class bias detection, choices-only baselines). S.U.R.E.'s specific instantiation (flagging `len(set(chosen_tool)) == 1` across ≥3 scenarios in an agentic tool-selection benchmark) is a novel, narrow application of a known general principle to a new task type (tool-routing, not MCQ), not the invention of artifact-aware evaluation itself. Recommend classifying this sub-claim as PARTIAL, not OPEN, in novelty_flags.json, and citing this paper as the general-principle precedent.

**State-of-the-art snapshot, Theme 6.** PSI is a decades-old metric originating in credit-scoring/risk-scorecard monitoring, now widely repurposed for general ML production drift monitoring (Arize, Fiddler, Coralogix, Evidently-style vendor literature all document it as a standard tool with the conventional thresholds <0.1 stable / 0.1–0.2 moderate / >0.2 significant drift). Critically, **the quantile (equal-frequency) vs. equal-width binning distinction that H5 centers on is already a documented, named methodological concern in the credit-scoring PSI literature** — multiple sources explicitly describe equal-frequency/quantile binning (each bin holding an equal share of the *reference* distribution, conventionally deciles) as the standard/recommended construction, and separately warn that "PSI is sensitive to binning choices [and] inappropriate binning may produce inaccurate or misleading results." 23. **PSI production-monitoring literature (Arize AI "Population Stability Index (PSI): What You Need To Know"; Fiddler AI "Measuring Data Drift with the Population Stability Index"; Coralogix "A Practical Introduction to PSI"; Arize "Data Binning Challenges in Production: How To Bin To Win").** Problem: quantifying distributional drift between a reference (training/baseline) population and a current (production) population for model-monitoring purposes. Core idea: PSI sums, over bins, `(actual% − expected%) × ln(actual%/expected%)`; conventional thresholds flag <0.1 as stable, 0.1–0.2 as moderate drift, >0.2 as significant drift warranting review/retrain. Binning-methods documentation explicitly distinguishes equal-width vs. equal-frequency (quantile) binning and states plainly that PSI results are sensitive to this choice and can be "inaccurate or misleading" under a poor binning scheme. Key results: this is presented uniformly across vendor sources as established best practice, not a research contribution. Limitations: mostly vendor blog/documentation tier, not peer-reviewed papers; originally designed for tabular/scorecard features (credit score, income), not vision-model detection-confidence outputs specifically. Relevance: **this is the single most important finding for H5's novelty status.** The equal-width-binning failure mode S.U.R.E. diagnosed (PSI jumping from 0.06 to 1.05 with no graded signal because several equal-width bins are empty in a narrow-range reference distribution) and its fix (quantile/decile binning) are **already a named, documented concern in the standard PSI/credit-scoring literature**, not a new discovery. **Recommend classifying H5 as PARTIAL-to-KNOWN, not OPEN, in novelty_flags.json**: the binning pitfall and its quantile-binning fix are established general PSI practice; S.U.R.E.'s contribution is narrower than "diagnosing and correcting a PSI binning failure" in the abstract — it is *documenting this known failure mode concretely occurring in a new, previously unexamined signal type* (YOLO detection-confidence distributions, which are narrow-banded for a different structural reason than credit scores) and wiring the fix into an automated three-way retrain-gating decision (`none`/`review`/`retrain`) for a vision-model MLOps pipeline, which is a legitimate but more modest contribution than the proposal's current framing implies.
24. **Drift-triggered vs. scheduled retraining practitioner literature (WWT "MLOps and Drift"; RandomTrees "Mastering Model Retraining in MLOps"; OneUptime "How to Configure Automated Model Retraining Triggered by Data Drift Alerts"; ResearchGate "AutoDrift: An End-to-End MLOps Pipeline with Automated Data Drift Detection and Triggered Model Retraining").** Problem: choosing between calendar-based and drift-triggered retraining policies. ### Batch 4 (searches 15–18): Theme 4 — RAG retrieval thresholding + Theme 5 — agentic tool-calling reliability (initial pass)

**State-of-the-art snapshot, Theme 4.** The e5 embedding family's asymmetric query/passage-prefix design (Wang et al., "Text Embeddings by Weakly-Supervised Contrastive Pre-training," 2022, arXiv:2212.03533) is well-established and independently confirmed as the reason e5-style models outperform symmetric sentence-similarity models (like the tr-bert baseline S.U.R.E. compares against) specifically as passage length grows — this is a strong external corroboration of a design choice S.U.R.E. already made and measured. Similarity-threshold calibration for RAG is an active 2025–2026 sub-literature with a consistent finding: there is no universal threshold, uncalibrated similarity scores are dataset-dependent (a 0.5 threshold's recall varies from 60–90% across datasets per one source), and practitioners must calibrate per-corpus against an explicit precision/recall or coverage target — exactly the exercise `rag/calibrate.py` performs.

15. **Wang, L. et al., "Text Embeddings by Weakly-Supervised Contrastive Pre-training" (E5), arXiv:2212.03533, 2022.** Problem: general-purpose text embeddings that generalize across retrieval tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. Method: contrastive pre-training on weakly-supervised text pairs at scale, with an explicit asymmetric "query:"/"passage:" prefix convention distinguishing short queries from long passages. Key results: E5 matches or beats models with far more parameters on MTEB/BEIR-style benchmarks; the asymmetric design is shown to matter specifically when query and passage differ substantially in length/style. Limitations: original paper is English-centric; multilingual variants (used implicitly here, since S.U.R.E.'s corpus is Turkish) are a later extension with less scrutiny. Relevance: directly explains, from first principles, why S.U.R.E.'s own benchmark (`rag/bench.py`) found e5-small beating tr-bert with a widening MRR gap as chunk/passage length grows (0.833 → 0.614) — the paper should cite this as the mechanism-level explanation for an empirical result S.U.R.E. already measured, not present the e5-vs-tr-bert gap as a novel discovery.
16. **RAG similarity-threshold calibration literature (ResearchGate "Evaluating Precision and Recall at Retrieval Time in RAG Systems," 2025; industry sources on threshold tuning, e.g., Braintrust "RAG evaluation metrics").** Problem: choosing a retrieval similarity threshold that balances hallucination risk (false-positive retrieval, i.e., returning an irrelevant chunk the model treats as grounding) against recall. Key results: reports thresholds in the 0.70–0.85 range preserving ~100% retention in one study, with sharp recall collapse above ~0.90–0.95; explicitly frames threshold choice as a deployment-priority decision (accuracy-prioritizing deployments use lower thresholds; efficiency-prioritizing ones tolerate higher thresholds and their accuracy cost) rather than a single "correct" value. Limitations: thresholds and their effects are corpus- and embedding-model-specific and not transferable across studies without recalibration — a point these sources make explicitly, which is itself relevant. Relevance: strongly supports H4's framing — S.U.R.E.'s explicit statement that its 0.85 threshold is scoped to "this 8-document/44-chunk Turkish-language corpus" is exactly the discipline this literature says is necessary and often skipped; this is good citable support for the *practice* of per-corpus calibration, while confirming the specific threshold value (0.85) is not itself a general finding.
17. **RAG hallucination-detection-via-embedding-similarity limits ("The Semantic Illusion: Certified Limits of Embedding-Based Hallucination Detection in RAG Systems," arXiv, 2025/2026; "HALT-RAG," arXiv:2509.07475).** Problem: whether embedding/similarity-based signals can reliably detect or prevent hallucination at all. Key results: "The Semantic Illusion" reportedly establishes formal/certified limits on how well embedding-similarity-based hallucination detection can work — i.e., similarity thresholding is not a complete solution, only a partial mitigation. Limitations: these are largely theoretical/formal-limit papers; practical implications for a specific deployed threshold (like S.U.R.E.'s 0.85) are not directly computed. Relevance: this is an important **honest caveat** for H4 — the paper should not claim the 0.85 threshold eliminates hallucination risk, only that it reduces one specific failure mode (fabricated citations from irrelevant retrieved chunks) at a measured recall cost, consistent with H2's already-stated scope limit that the deterministic-override principle does not extend to open-ended judgment failures (a hallucinated but plausible-sounding synthesis from a correctly-retrieved chunk is not addressed by a similarity threshold at all).
18. **Structure-aware / heading-based chunking evaluation (arXiv:2512.00367 "Breaking It Down: Domain-Aware Semantic Segmentation for RAG"; arXiv:2603.24556 chunking evaluation for oil & gas enterprise documents; arXiv:2504.19754 "Reconstructing Context").** Problem: comparing chunking strategies (fixed-size, recursive, semantic/breakpoint-based, structure-aware) for RAG retrieval quality and cost. ### Batch 3 (searches 9–14): Theme 3 — LLM safety/guardrails in safety-critical systems + PRIOR-ART CHECK on "deterministic final authority"

**State-of-the-art snapshot / ADVERSARIAL FINDING (important, report honestly per instructions).** The specific pattern S.U.R.E. calls "the model never has the final word; a deterministic rule engine is the single source of truth, escalating but never lowering severity" is **not a new pattern in the abstract** — it is a well-established design principle appearing independently under at least three different names in three different literatures that predate this system by years to decades:
(a) **Safety Instrumented Systems (SIS) / interlocks** in classical process-safety/industrial-automation engineering (IEC 61508/61511-adjacent practice): an SIS is explicitly defined as an independent, deterministic logic-solver layer that is "the last line of defense," sitting downstream of and overriding a (traditionally non-probabilistic, but structurally analogous) Basic Process Control System, with the explicit property that it forces the process to a safe state when a threshold is breached — this is a decades-old, industry-standard formalization of "deterministic component has final, override authority over an upstream reasoning/control layer," just not originally framed around an LLM.
(b) **Shielding in safe reinforcement learning**: a "shield" is formally defined as an external runtime filter that vetoes or corrects an agent's proposed actions before execution to guarantee compliance with a safety specification ("post-decision shielding... vetoing and correcting any unsafe action") — this is structurally identical to S.U.R.E.'s escalate-only override (a deterministic component sits between a probabilistic decision-maker and the executed/reported outcome), formalized in the RL-safety literature well before LLM-specific guardrail discourse.
(c) **2025–2026 LLM-guardrail industry/practitioner writing** has independently converged on almost the exact same slogan and mechanism S.U.R.E. uses: "the LLM handles the creative, flexible part... the rules engine handles the deterministic, must-not-fail part... LLM generates → rules engine validates → if validation passes, deliver" (Rulebricks, "Deterministic Guardrails for LLMs"; similar framing in the Medium/dev.to/Arthur.ai sources found). This is close enough in wording and mechanism that S.U.R.E.'s own "the model proposes, the rule engine disposes" refrain should be read as *articulating a pattern already circulating in 2025–2026 applied-AI practitioner literature*, not coining it.
(d) **Emerging 2024–2025 academic literature on LLM agents specifically in industrial process control** (arXiv:2409.18009 "Control Industrial Automation System with LLM Agents"; arXiv:2507.07115; ETFA 2025 fault-tolerant-control agent work) explicitly proposes "every [LLM] proposal checked by an external validator before actuation" as the safety pattern for LLM-driven industrial control — this is the closest same-domain-class (industrial LLM agent + external deterministic check) prior art found, though it is aimed at general industrial process control/fault recovery, not aquaculture, and (per abstracts found) is validated in simulation/framework-design papers rather than as an already-deployed, empirically measured production system with a documented eval-harness-drift near-miss.

11. **"Safety instrumented system," background synthesis from Wikipedia, AIChE Beacon, Control Engineering, ISA InTech (multiple sources, industrial safety engineering literature).** Problem addressed: how to guarantee a process is forced to a safe state independent of the (fallible) primary control/reasoning system. Core idea: an SIS is architecturally and physically independent from the Basic Process Control System (BPCS), with its own sensors/logic-solver/final-control-elements, and functions purely as an override/shutdown layer triggered by threshold breaches. Key results/claims: described industry-wide as "the last line of defense," standard practice across oil & gas, chemical, nuclear, power generation. Assumptions/limitations: designed for hardware/physical-process control, not originally for arbitrating between a probabilistic reasoning model and a rule set; independence is typically physical/electrical, not just logical/code-level. Relevance to S.U.R.E.: **this is the closest EQUIVALENT_KNOWN prior art for the "deterministic final authority" pattern**, under an entirely different name and in a different literature (industrial control engineering, not ML). It must be cited in Related Work, and the paper's novelty claim must be explicitly reframed relative to it: S.U.R.E. is not inventing the override-authority pattern, but applying a *software-only, LLM-specific instantiation* of an SIS-like discipline (with literal code-sharing between production and evaluation as the integrity mechanism, rather than physical sensor/actuator independence) to a new domain (aquaculture RAS) and a new upstream component type (a local LLM rather than a BPCS).
12. **"Shields for Safe Reinforcement Learning," *Communications of the ACM*, 2024/2025; "Verification-Guided Shielding for Deep Reinforcement Learning," arXiv, 2024; "Watchdogs and Oracles: Runtime Verification Meets Large Language Models for Autonomous Systems," arXiv, 2025.** Problem: guaranteeing formal safety properties for RL agents (and, in the "Watchdogs and Oracles" paper, extending runtime verification specifically to LLM-driven autonomous systems) whose internal policies are not directly verifiable. Core idea: a shield/runtime monitor observes the agent's proposed action and vetoes/corrects it before execution if it would violate a safety specification; "Watchdogs and Oracles" explicitly motivates this for LLMs, noting their internal reasoning is opaque, making runtime (rather than pre-deployment/static) verification the practical safety mechanism. Key results: shields provide provable safety guarantees under a formal safety spec; the LLM-specific runtime-verification framing is recent (2025) and still largely conceptual/framework-level in the sources found. Limitations: shielding formalisms generally assume an enumerable, specifiable action/state space — directly mirroring S.U.R.E.'s own H2 boundary condition (works for enumerable thresholds, not open-ended judgment). Relevance: **directly relevant EQUIVALENT_KNOWN prior art**, and notably the shielding literature's own scope limitation (needs a formal, enumerable specification) is the same boundary S.U.R.E.'s H2 states from first-hand system observation — this convergence is worth citing as independent theoretical corroboration of H2's claim, strengthening rather than weakening the paper.
13. **Rulebricks, "Deterministic Guardrails for LLMs: Building Safe, Auditable AI Systems" (2025/2026); "Deterministic Guardrails for Non-Deterministic Agents," dev.to (2026); Arthur.ai, "AI Agent Guardrails: Pre-LLM & Post-LLM Best Practices."** Problem: practitioner guidance for combining LLMs with deterministic validation in production agent systems. Core idea: "LLM generates → rules engine validates → if validation passes, deliver," with the rules engine holding authority over compliance/safety-critical decisions (PII, schema, hard boundaries) while the LLM retains only the natural-language/reasoning role. Key claims: framed as current (2025–2026) industry best practice, not a novel research contribution in these sources. Limitations: blog/practitioner-tier sources, not peer-reviewed; typically describe SaaS/fintech-style guardrails (PII masking, schema validation), not physical safety-critical domains with life/stock-loss stakes, and rarely describe a literal code-shared production/evaluation-harness integrity mechanism of the kind S.U.R.E.'s eval-harness-drift incident motivates. Relevance: confirms the "LLM proposes, deterministic layer disposes" framing is **already circulating as an applied-AI industry pattern-name** as of this writing — the paper should not present this framing as coined by S.U.R.E., but can legitimately claim it is a rare *documented, measured, deployed instance* of the pattern in a physical safety-critical (rather than compliance/SaaS) domain.
14. **Human-in-the-loop LLM hallucination mitigation in clinical decision support (Communications Medicine 2025 "Multi-model assurance analysis... adversarial hallucination attacks during clinical decision support"; BMC Health Services Research 2026 systematic review; multiple arXiv sources on medical LLM hallucination).** Problem: LLMs used for clinical decision support hallucinate (fabricated citations, incorrect treatment claims) with patient-safety consequences. Relevance: HITL is the medical-domain's dominant analogous pattern to S.U.R.E.'s deterministic override, but is a materially different mechanism (a human decides, not a rule); this is useful contrastive Related Work — S.U.R.E.'s contribution can be framed as substituting a *deterministic, always-available, code-tested* backstop for the *human-availability-constrained* HITL pattern dominant in the clinical-LLM literature, which matters specifically for a 24/7 unattended RAS deployment.

### Batch 2 (searches 5–8): Theme 2 — Edge AI deployment for vision models

**State-of-the-art snapshot.** CoreML/Apple Neural Engine deployment of YOLO-family detectors is a well-documented, actively benchmarked path (Ultralytics ships first-class CoreML export docs), with quantization (INT8, FP16, and newer 4-bit block-wise/palettization schemes on recent macOS) treated as a standard lever. The accuracy-cost literature for TensorRT/GPU-class INT8 quantization is much richer than for CoreML/ANE specifically, reporting typical INT8 mAP costs in the 0.5–2.0pp (TensorRT calibrated) to 3–7% (uncalibrated/mAP50-95, harsher metric) range — S.U.R.E.'s reported INT8 ΔmAP50 of −0.0082 sits comfortably inside or below this typical range. A distinct, less commonly reported theme is exactly S.U.R.E.'s H6 finding: several practitioner reports (GitHub issues on Ultralytics/ONNX/ONNX Runtime repos) independently confirm that NMS/post-processing operations do not export cleanly from PyTorch to ONNX/TensorRT and are a documented source of accuracy/behavior discrepancy between export formats — this is corroborating, not just supporting, prior art for H6's mechanism claim (loss is post-processing-attributable, not a numeric-precision artifact of any one export path).

7. **Ultralytics, "CoreML Export for YOLO Models," Ultralytics Docs (ongoing, 2025/2026 revision).** Problem/scope: reference documentation for exporting YOLO models to CoreML for Apple Neural Engine deployment, including FP16/INT8 export options. Key claims: YOLO11 quantized on iPhone ANE reportedly reaches 60+ FPS for live video (vs. 200+ FPS on discrete GPU at FP32/FP16 for the same family on COCO). Limitations: vendor documentation, not independently peer-reviewed; figures are marketing-adjacent and hardware/version-specific. Relevance: establishes CoreML/ANE-YOLO deployment as an actively supported, mainstream path (not a novel integration by S.U.R.E.), and gives an independent (if rough) FPS reference point against which S.U.R.E.'s 111 FPS / 9.0 ms p50 on an M4 Pro for YOLOv11s can be read as plausible and consistent with, not anomalous relative to, the vendor's own reported order of magnitude.
8. **GitHub issue threads: `ultralytics/yolov5#8772` ("Poor results when detecting object with exported model (onnx, torchscript)"), `onnx/onnx#6287` and `microsoft/onnxruntime#21689` ("Inference time discrepancy... TorchScript vs ONNX").** Problem: practitioner-reported accuracy/behavior discrepancies between PyTorch-native and exported (ONNX/TorchScript) object-detection models. Key finding (paraphrased from search synthesis): NMS and thresholding post-processing steps do not export seamlessly from PyTorch to ONNX and compiled TensorRT graphs, and output tensor formats vary across export configurations — a repeatedly independently observed, community-documented root cause. Limitations: not peer-reviewed, anecdotal/issue-tracker evidence rather than controlled study; specific to YOLO-family Ultralytics export tooling. Relevance: this is the single most directly relevant piece of prior art against H6 — it is essentially the same causal mechanism S.U.R.E.'s `export_bench.py` diagnoses (post-processing/NMS path, not weight precision, causes the export accuracy loss), independently observed by other practitioners in a different but related toolchain. **This should be cited explicitly in Related Work as prior, informal community evidence for the same mechanism**, which strengthens rather than undermines H6: S.U.R.E.'s contribution is not discovering that NMS-export mismatch exists (community already suspected this), but rigorously *quantifying and formally attributing* it via a controlled, same-validation-set, six-configuration benchmark — a methodological rather than a discovery-level novelty, which should be stated honestly.
9. **[Multiple sources synthesized] TensorRT/ONNX Runtime INT8 quantization benchmarking practice (Ultralytics Academy "Optimize with TensorRT"; NVIDIA TensorRT quantization guidance; arXiv:2508.19600 "Quantization Robustness to Input Degradations for Object Detection").** Problem: characterizing accuracy-latency-size trade-offs of INT8 quantization for detectors on GPU/edge hardware. Key results: reports typical calibrated-INT8 accuracy costs of 0.5–2.0pp (TensorRT, well-calibrated) up to 3–7% mAP50-95 drop (less carefully calibrated/other studies); recommends re-calibration if losses exceed ~0.5 mAP (FP16) / ~1.0 mAP (INT8). Limitations: mostly GPU/TensorRT-centric, not CoreML/ANE-specific; calibration-set size and methodology vary widely across sources, making cross-study comparison approximate. Relevance: gives an external reference band against which to state S.U.R.E.'s INT8 ΔmAP50 (−0.0082, i.e., ~0.8pp on mAP50) as within normal/expected bounds for careful calibration — useful for Experimental Setup & Edge Metrics section framing, and also supports (rather than contradicts) H6's claim that this loss is smaller than the fp32 export-path loss already paid (−0.0104), which is the less commonly discussed but directly relevant comparison this literature rarely makes explicit (most papers compare INT8-to-fp32-native, not INT8-to-fp32-exported).
10. **Cloud vs. edge inference practitioner/field studies (Roboflow "Comparing Cloud and On-Device Inference for Computer Vision"; agricultural field-latency study reporting 347–383 ms average edge latency advantage; TechAhead decision-model post).** Problem: quantifying when edge inference is preferable to cloud inference for real-time monitoring. Key results: edge inference is favored when sub-100 ms latency is required or connectivity is unreliable/absent, consistent with a field agricultural study reporting edge latency of 347–383 ms average with lower variance vs. cloud round-trips of 50–500 ms. Limitations: agricultural field study appears to concern coarser-grained agri-monitoring tasks, not millisecond-scale detector inference; numbers are not directly comparable across studies due to differing pipelines. Relevance: supports S.U.R.E.'s architectural choice to run vision (and the LLM) fully on-prem/edge rather than cloud-hosted as consistent with the connectivity-constrained agricultural deployment literature broadly, though S.U.R.E.'s own 9.0 ms figure is an order of magnitude faster than the cited field-latency numbers because it measures only on-device model inference, not a full network+processing round trip — this distinction should be stated explicitly to avoid an apples-to-oranges comparison in Related Work.

**Gap this S.U.R.E. paper's contribution addresses:** the literature confirms CoreML/ANE-YOLO deployment and INT8 quantization benchmarking are both mainstream, well-trodden practices — S.U.R.E. does not invent either. What is comparatively rare in the reviewed literature is (a) holding the *entire* validation methodology (same val set, same percentile-latency protocol) constant across *every* export target in one controlled sweep, and (b) explicitly separating and quantifying the fp32-export-path loss from the quantization-added loss — most sources compare INT8 only against the fp32-*native* (untouched) baseline, not against the fp32-*exported* baseline, which is exactly the comparison that makes H6's "quantization is not where you pay for edge speed" claim visible. The GitHub-issue-level community evidence (item 8) means the mechanism is informally known but, per this search, not previously formalized into a quantitative, same-corpus, cross-format attribution study of the kind `export_bench.py` performs — this is a defensible methodological novelty claim, not a discovery-of-a-new-phenomenon claim.
